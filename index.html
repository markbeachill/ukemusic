    How the chord sheet looks
    
                      
    
                        
                        
                        
    The first is the classic chord-sheet look. Columns keep chords above their words with ordinary fonts, but space words out more. Chords in the words takes the least room and suits a phone. Chords only shows each line's chords, for whoever is accompanying the singer.
    
                      
    
                      
    
                        
                        
                        
    The default for every song. A song can have its own choice: Export → Section names. Hidden, the sections are just a line apart; a heading with nothing under it (a cue like "Chorus x2") always stays.
    
                      
    
                      
    
                        
                        
                        
    The default for every song (a song can have its own: View → The tune). The strip shows the verse tune, the chorus tune and so on once each, so the sheet stays short. Over every line puts a staff above each line it's sung on: the most like a songbook, but about twice as long. Stage copies never show it.
    
                      
    
                      
    
                        
                        
                        
    The boxes show the time signature, the pattern's name, arrows, letters and the count (1 & 2 &). They're sized from the sheet's font and print clearly in black and white. Write {3/4 D-DUDU} to change the time from there on.

                        Voice (Play and Karaoke)
                        
                        
                        
                        
    Uses your computer's own voice. Chords are the shapes you play (with a capo, the capo shapes).
    
                      
    
                      
    
                        
                        
                        
    Swung, the beats stay put and each in-between strum (the "&") comes a little late, so the strum goes long-short. The tempo doesn't change. In a song, {swing D-DU-UDU}, {light swing …}, {shuffle …} or {straight …} sets it from there on. 6/8 and triplet patterns lilt already and aren't changed.

            The tab, in steps
            
      
            This song has its own tab, so it's played as written (the picking pattern is only for the chords). Tab is easier to learn in layers: the chords under it first, then your picking hand on its own, then both together, slowly before in time. Each step plays in karaoke; tap a line to start from there.
            
      
            
      
            
      
            
                

    
      
            
        
            Write it out as a new song
            
        
            This makes a new song in your library with every picked line written out as tab, the chords over it and the words under it. It's yours to edit: change any note, add a fill, change a bar. It plays exactly as written (no pattern any more).
            
        
            The original song isn't changed, and the two are separate from now on: a change to one doesn't reach the other. To try another pattern, write it out again as another song.

            How picking works
            
      
            
        
            Picking to sing along to. Your left hand holds the chords just as it does to strum. Your right hand picks the strings one (or two) at a time, in a pattern, instead of brushing across them all. Choose a pattern here and the song plays picked; the strip at the top of karaoke shows each string as it comes.
            
        
            This is accompaniment: the tune is your voice's job, just as when you strum. Published picking for songs like House of the Rising Sun or Scarborough Fair is just this. A picked solo (a "chord melody", where the tune itself is on the top strings) is different, and needs the tune written out: the Chord melody pattern makes one when the song has its tune written in (below).
            
        
            Singing over it. Get the pattern going on its own first, until you can play it while thinking about something else; then add the words. The easier patterns leave more of you free to sing. Picking is quieter than strumming, so find your first note before you start: set it in Info → First note and it's sounded with the first click of the count-in (or press First note here).
            
        
            The fingers. On a ukulele each finger has its own string: the thumb (p) plays g, the index finger (i) C, the middle finger (m) E and the ring finger (a) A. The letters are the usual ones in fingerpicking books. On a guitar the thumb takes the chord's bass note and the fingers the top three strings.
            
        
            The patterns, easiest first:
            
        
              
          
            1. One string a beat (g C E A): slow and steady, to get used to holding a chord while you pick.
              2. 
          
            2. Up, twice a bar (g C E A g C E A): the same, twice as fast.
              3. 
          
            3. Up and back (g C E A E C): across and back. Lovely in 3/4 and 6/8.
              4. 
          
            4. Pinch and roll (g+A C E C): thumb and ring finger together on the beat.
              5. 
          
            5. Alternating thumb (Travis): the thumb on every beat, swapping g and C, the fingers in between.
              6. 
          
            6. Chord melody: a picked solo, the tune itself on top, when the song has its tune written in (see below).
              7. 
        
            
        
            Tab and chord boxes. Tab is the usual way to write picking: one line per string (the top line is the A string), and a number says which fret to hold as you pick that string (0 is open). Chord boxes show the shape for your left hand. With picked chords, the two go together: hold the whole chord shape, and every number in the tab is already under a finger. So you read the chord, and let the tab tell your right hand which strings and when.
            
        
            The pick sheet sets the song out like a fingerstyle songbook: the pattern counted out, the chord boxes, then each line as tab with the chord over the bar where it starts and the words underneath. It's in the song's learn folder, and in Export.
            
        
            Changing the pattern part-way. The pattern chosen here is where the song starts. To change it for a verse, a chorus or a single line, put the cursor there and use Change here: it adds a line to the song, which you can edit like a strum line:
            
        
            {pick: up and back}        a pattern by name
{pick: g C E A E C}        your own, by string (g+A together, - a rest)
{pick: p i m a m i}        your own, by finger
{pick V: g+A C E C}        named V, to use again later with {pick V}
{pick: strum}              back to strumming
            
        
            The chords stay chords, so you can still change them, move the key and use Learn. Your own pattern repeats to fill each bar (8 steps in 4/4, 6 in 3/4).
            
        
            Changing single notes. For a fill or a reworked bar, put the cursor on the line and choose Write out ▾ → This line: just that line becomes tab, here in the song, and every note of it is yours to change; the rest stays chords and patterns. Edit → Undo takes it back.
            
        
            Write it out to change it. A pattern is the same everywhere; to make a whole fixed arrangement you can rework anywhere, choose Write out ▾ → The whole song. It makes a new song in your library with every picked line written out as tab (chords over it, words under it), in a ```tab 4/4 block that plays exactly as written. Edit it like any other song. The original isn't changed, and the two stay separate from then on.
            
                
            Songs with their own tab. Tab written in the song (an intro, a riff) is always played as written; the pattern is only for the chords around it. Tab in steps… helps you learn that tab: the chords under it, then the picking on open strings, then both.
            
        
            The tune, and chord melody. A song can carry its tune, a line at a time, on a line of its own just above the words:
            
        
            {melody: E E F G | G F E D | C C D E | E. D/2 D2}
[C]Joyful, joyful, [G7]we adore Thee, [C]God of glory, [G7]Lord of love;
            
        
            Write the notes by name (E, F#, Bb), in the song's written key. Each note lasts a beat unless it says otherwise: E2 two beats, E/2 half a beat, E. a beat and a half; r is a rest. Each note is taken as the nearest one to the note before; add ' for an octave higher or , for lower. The bars (|) just make it easier to read; a short first bar is a pickup (the "A-" of "A-mazing"). A line without a tune of its own borrows the tune of an earlier line with the same chords, so you usually only write the first verse. If you have a tune in ABC (the folk tune format), write {melody abc: …} instead.
            
        
            With a tune, Play the tune plays it quietly over the chords while you learn the song, and the Chord melody pattern turns it into a picked solo: the tune on the top strings, picked from the chord you're holding, with a chord note under it on beat 1. Tune notes higher than about the 7th fret are a stretch: try the song in another key.
            
        
            Learning it. Hold one chord and play the pattern until your picking hand does it on its own. Then change chords on beat 1. Then the song, slowly (Play → Practice speed), a little faster each day. Then sing over it.
            
      
            
      
            
                

    
      
            The circle of fifths
            
      
            Twelve chords round the outside, each a fifth from the next, and their relative minors inside. It's turned so the song's home chord is at the top: chords next to it belong with it, and music loves to move one step anticlockwise, towards home.
