# Six beginner songs for ukulele or guitar

Six separate `.cho` files, numbered in the suggested teaching order. Each has a key, time signature, suggested practice tempo, chord changes, written-out repeats and a starting strum. They are short teaching versions, except that Auld Lang Syne includes all five verses. They are fresh, deliberately simple accompaniments rather than transcriptions of a recording. The chords work for either instrument; use that instrument's own shapes.

In ukemusic, use **File → Import text file** for a single file, or **Import ChordPro files…** for this folder. The folder importer marks imported songs as personal use by default; it does not infer public-domain status from these notes. Review the song's sharing setting before exporting a library pack. The six songs are also in the Public Domain Songs library, with these notes in each song's notes.

`DDDD` means four even down-strums in a full bar of 4/4. First learn the changes with one strum on each new chord. Repeated chord names help show the shape of each line; they do not necessarily mean a new chord. ChordPro positions chords against words, but these files do not encode exact bar lengths, melody or karaoke timing. Use the familiar sung tune to place the changes, or have a leader demonstrate it. The tempos are practice suggestions, not historical claims.

Read [Teaching through a small song collection](Teaching%20through%20a%20small%20song%20collection.md) for the lesson structure, instrument adjustments and a sample session.

## Words, sources and reuse

The selected underlying words are public domain; the newly written teaching text and chord accompaniments use the repository's [MIT licence](../../LICENSE). No modern recording, solo, piano accompaniment or published guitar/ukulele arrangement has been copied. Source descriptions below distinguish historical evidence from a transcription: not every linked history page is the exact word-for-word edition used.

1. **Buffalo Gals:** selected traditional verse and chorus, with spelling modernised and “walking” replacing “lumb'ring”. The Library of Congress [history and linked 1848 sheet music](https://blogs.loc.gov/folklife/2020/08/buffalo-gals-revisited-2/) documents early versions; an [1890 newspaper reproduction](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Buffalo_Gals.jpg) supplies a historical text witness. The chorus retains “can't you come out”, rather than the familiar alternative “won't you come out”. The song's history includes blackface minstrelsy; this file retains the street-and-dance verse only.
2. **Drunken Sailor:** verses 1–3 and the refrain of Richard Runciman Terry's [The Shanty Book, Part I (1921), no. 14](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20774/pg20774-images.html#Page_30). Repeated lines are written out and pronunciation marks omitted. Terry died in 1938. His piano accompaniment is not reproduced. The old words describe rough treatment; choose a different song if that is unsuitable for a particular group.
3. **Oh! Susanna:** Stephen Foster (1826–1864), published 1848. [Library of Congress historical score record](https://www.loc.gov/item/2023800745/) and [University of Pittsburgh song record](https://americanmusic.library.pitt.edu/content/oh-susanna). Selected first and dream verses only, with spelling modernised. The racist verse is omitted. These catalogue records establish historical editions; their scans were not available for line-by-line checking during preparation. This is a selected, normalised teaching text, not a diplomatic transcription.
4. **Down by the Riverside:** traditional African American spiritual, using the sword-and-shield and burden verses and the familiar refrain. [Hymnary text and public-domain attribution](https://hymnary.org/text/gonna_lay_down_my_sword_and_shield). Phrases are arranged into the familiar repeated verse/chorus form; no modern harmonisation is used. The [Tabernacle Choir history](https://www.thetabernaclechoir.org/articles/down-by-the-riverside-history.html?lang=eng) identifies a 1918 publication in Plantation Melodies; its earlier history is not needed to establish this teaching version's attribution.
5. **When the Saints Go Marching In:** traditional African American spiritual. Selected verses 1, 2 and 4 from the [Hymnary text, marked public domain](https://www.hymnary.org/text/o_when_the_saints_go_marching_in), with “O” written as “Oh” and the opening verse repeated at the end. The [Library of Congress essay by Ricky Riccardi](https://www.loc.gov/static/programs/national-recording-preservation-board/documents/When-the-Saint-Go-Marching-In_Riccardi.pdf) distinguishes the traditional song from the differently worded 1896 hymn and from Armstrong's recorded interpretation. No Armstrong introduction or instrumental material is included.
6. **Auld Lang Syne:** Robert Burns (1759–1796), traditional Scottish tune. Words follow [The Poetical Works of Robert Burns (1887), Auld Lang Syne](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Poetical_Works_of_Robert_Burns/Auld_Lang_Syne), retaining that edition's five-verse order and Scots vocabulary. Each chorus is printed in full. See the [Morgan Library account](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/Auld-Lang-Syne/8) for the familiar tune's publication in 1799.

Sources consulted 7 November 2026. Public-domain status of a traditional song does not extend to every later arrangement, translation or recording. These notes concern the selected texts and these new chord sheets.
