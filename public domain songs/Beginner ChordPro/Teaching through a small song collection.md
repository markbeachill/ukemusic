# Teaching through a small song collection

## The idea

A small songbook can act as a course when each song gives the player a clear next step. The aim is to finish recognisable music while learning one manageable skill at a time: changing between two shapes, keeping a pulse, adding a third chord, following a chorus, or playing without watching every prompt.

The inspiration comes from three practical examples. The [Tampa Bay Ukulele Society beginner book](https://www.tampabayukulele.com/uploads/1/0/8/4/108415519/tbus_beginnersbook2015.pdf) includes a table of chords and short change exercises. [Fretwork's twelve-song book](https://guitarfretwork.com/printables/easy-guitar-songbook/) orders pieces from easier to harder and pairs chords with a strum. The [School Ukulele Orchestra book](https://school-ukulele-orchestra.net/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/ukulele-accompaniment-book-2021-edited.pdf) puts chord diagrams beside songs and offers some alternative keys. Together these suggest a useful structure: show the shapes, rehearse a change, then put it straight into a song.

The sequence below is a proposal for these six new sheets, not a tested teaching programme or a reproduction of any of those books. Their song permissions must be considered separately from their useful teaching ideas.

## A route through the six songs

| Song | Chords in this sheet | Main teaching purpose | A useful sign of progress |
|---|---|---|---|
| Buffalo Gals | C, G7 | Make one familiar change and return home | Sing the chorus twice without stopping at a change |
| Drunken Sailor | Am, G | Repeat a pattern while hearing a minor sound | Keep the pulse through the last line's quicker return to Am |
| Oh! Susanna | C, F, G7 | Introduce F at a recognisable chorus | Move from verse to chorus without losing the beat |
| Down by the Riverside | C, F, G7 | Reuse those shapes in a different phrase pattern | Play the chorus with even down-strums |
| When the Saints Go Marching In | C, F, G7 | Enter after pickup words and hear G7 lead home | Start with the group and land together on the final C |
| Auld Lang Syne | C, F, G7 | Sustain a slower pulse and remember longer phrases | Play one verse and chorus with fewer glances at the sheet |

This is an order of musical tasks, not a universal ranking of finger difficulty. On standard ukulele, Am is easy but G needs coordination. On guitar, F may be the biggest obstacle: start with a small F shape on the top four strings, avoiding the unused strings, or transpose to a more comfortable key and use a capo if appropriate. Baritone ukulele needs its own shapes too. A key that suits the singer matters more than keeping every learner in C.

## How a lesson works

**Hear and sing first.** The leader sings a short phrase while the group taps the beat. This establishes the tune, the pickup and the ending before the player's attention goes to their fingers. If a tune is unfamiliar, demonstrate it; a chord sheet alone cannot teach its melody.

**Isolate one change.** Find the pair that causes hesitation. Move slowly between those shapes, first silently and then with one strum per change. In Oh! Susanna, practise C to F and back before adding the whole chorus. Use ukemusic's chord-change practice or a simple repeated pair.

**Play with the least possible rhythm.** Begin with one down-strum at each chord change while the leader carries the beat. Then try four even down-strums per full bar. Add an up-strum or a gentle swing only when the changes are comfortable. A suggested tempo is a starting point; lowering it is part of learning.

**Return to the song quickly.** Loop the troublesome phrase a few times, then play the complete verse or chorus. This lets the player hear why the exercise mattered. Keep familiar songs in later sessions so progress includes ease and confidence, as well as new material.

**Remove a little support.** Once a phrase is secure, look away for one line, say the next chord before playing it, or use ukemusic's fading-chord Learn option. Finish with a complete musical performance, even if that means singing just one verse and chorus slowly.

## A twenty-minute group session

Spend three minutes singing and tapping a familiar chorus, four minutes rehearsing one change, five minutes playing a new verse and chorus with plain down-strums, four minutes repeating one awkward phrase, and four minutes playing a familiar song together. Agree a count-in and a clear final stop. Players who lose their place can keep tapping and rejoin at the next chorus.

Judge progress by steadier rhythm, easier changes and successful re-entry, rather than speed alone. These are working cues, not examination thresholds. A learner may need several sessions on two chords; another may be ready for the same song in a new key.

## Using the sheets in ukemusic

Import the ChordPro files and arrange them in a beginner setlist. Use slow playback, chord-change practice, phrase loops and Learn as appropriate. These portable sheets contain chord positions and practice suggestions, but no exact melody or bar-duration data. Check the imported playback against the sung tune before relying on its timing; a leader can teach directly from the sheets meanwhile.

The existing collection supplies the next steps: Amazing Grace introduces a three-beat sway, The Wild Rover supports a group chorus, and Scarborough Fair gives a contrasting minor/modal sound. The six new songs therefore provide an entrance to the collection and several chances to reuse the same skills.
